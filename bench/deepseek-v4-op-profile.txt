One GB10, no RPC, no model loaded for the measurement itself. This file records how to get a per-op
profile on this pair without `perf` or `nsys`, and what the first such profile says about the 4-bit
two-node decode.

Why this route: `perf` is blocked (`perf_event_paranoid`), `ptrace` is denied so `strace` cannot
attach, and an unbounded `nsys` capture of a tensor-parallel decode came out 1.5 GB. Upstream ships a
pair of tests that between them do the job offline:

    cmake --build build -j16 --target test-export-graph-ops test-backend-ops
    ./build/bin/test-export-graph-ops -m <model.gguf> -c 512 -np 6 -ngl 0 -o .scratch/gq4.txt
    ./build/bin/test-backend-ops perf --test-file .scratch/gq4.txt -b CUDA0 -o MUL_MAT,MUL_MAT_ID

`test-export-graph-ops` loads the model metadata only (`set_tensor_data` is a noop, so weights are
never read), reserves the prompt and generation graphs and writes their op descriptions out.
`test-backend-ops perf --test-file` then allocates tensors of those exact shapes on the chosen backend
and times each op. With `-np 6` the generation graph is the six-token batch a DSpark verify uses.
Console output is the useful format; `--output csv` in this tree carries no timing column.
Exporting the 144.44 GiB UD-Q4_K_XL takes about a minute because nothing but metadata is touched, so
this is a seconds-scale loop where a model load is 17 minutes.

Model: DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 UD-Q4_K_XL, 43 blocks, n_embd 4096, 256 experts, top-6, expert ffn
2048. 8454 nodes, 270 unique ops. What the report prints is bytes moved per run and GB/s, which is the
achieved bandwidth of that op.

What the profile says:

- Every op lands on a floor near 10 us whatever its size: 369 kB takes 10.05 us and 4472 kB takes
  11.01 us. That floor is per-op latency inside a replayed CUDA graph, not bandwidth. The generation
  graph has roughly a thousand executable ops, so on the order of 10 ms of a pass is this floor.
- The large dense projections run at 205 to 220 GB/s, against the 232 GB/s this pair measures for a
  five-token pass and 273 GB/s of peak. There is no bandwidth headroom left in `attn_wo_a`,
  `attn_out`, `node_19` or the lm head.
- The lm head is the single most expensive op in the graph: 2.4 ms per pass for six output rows,
  because it re-reads its 552 MB of q8_0 weights. It appears once for the target verify and five more
  times for the draft's full-vocabulary ranking, which is most of the draft's measured 20.6 ms.
- The rows above 230 GB/s are L2 effects, not real: each op is re-run in a tight loop, so its weights
  stay resident. Values up to several TB/s appear that way. Treat the small-op and MoE rows as
  latency and shape information only.

Caveats that matter before drawing conclusions:

- The exporter writes shapes, not data, so `MUL_MAT_ID` draws random expert ids. A real six-token
  batch from one sequence shares experts between tokens; random ids do not, so this profile touches
  more distinct experts than a real verify does.
- The replay cannot see the coordination: no RPC round trip, no allreduce, no sampling, no draft
  scheduling. The sum of the timed ops is therefore a floor for the pass, not the pass.

What it rules out: a better kernel for the expert matvecs at this batch size. The incremental slope of
the two-node arm is 8.75 ms per extra token (43 ms at one token, 78 at five, from
`bench/deepseek-v4-tensor-parallel-async.txt`), against 7.4 ms per node predicted for six experts per
token per layer at 232 GB/s, so the MoE path is already at roughly 85 % of what the part can move. The
dense path is at 205 to 220 GB/s on the big tensors. Both are close to the memory system, which is why
the remaining gap is not in the matmul kernels.

What it points to, with the earlier per-pass accounting (131 ms per pass, 110 ms of it verify, 20.6 ms
draft, 4.50 tokens accepted): the fixed cost of a pass. At the measured marginal of 8.75 ms per token,
a 4.50-token pass should cost about 39 ms of weight traffic; it costs 131 ms. The difference is per-op
latency floors, the 2.4 ms lm head, the draft's full-vocabulary rankings, and the coordination the
replay cannot see (~87 allreduces and the subgraph round trips). That is where the next engineering
goes, and this route makes each attempt measurable in seconds instead of a 17-minute load.

--- 2026-09-14, a per-pass budget: multiplicities joined to the replay ---

The replay above times one occurrence of each *unique* op, and the generation graph has 5208
non-view nodes, so the times only become a pass budget once each signature is multiplied by how often
it appears. The exporter now prints that: `tests/test-export-graph-ops.cpp` gained a multiplicity
counter (local change, `MULT <count> <op> <type> <ne...>` on stderr, one line per signature), and
`scripts/op-budget.py` joins the two files. Costs a minute to export and two to replay, no model load.

Grep of the exporter's multiplicities, six-token generation graph: 133 unique signatures, 5208 nodes.
Budget for one pass on one device, from the replay times (L2-favourable, see the caveat above):

| op family | nodes | ms/pass | share of timed |
|---|---|---|---|
| `MUL_MAT` (dense projections) | 683 | 22.3 | 52 % |
| `MUL_MAT_ID` (MoE) | 86 | 16.5 | 39 % |
| `CONCAT` | 374 | 2.0 | 5 % |
| `RMS_NORM` | 365 | 0.8 | 2 % |
| `MUL`, `ADD`, `SUM_ROWS`, `DIV`, `SQRT`, `REPEAT` | 1500 | 1.1 | 3 % |
| total timed | | 42.7 | |

Largest single signatures: the MoE at 16.5 ms (86 nodes, `ne=[2048,6,6,1]`), the MLA output
projections at 6.8 ms (`ne=[32768,6,1,1]`, 43 nodes) and 6.7 ms (`ne=[1024,6,8,1]`, 43 nodes), and the
lm head at 2.3 ms for its single node. 2434 of the 5208 nodes have no timing line in the replay (cases
test-backend-ops skips or that this join does not parse), so 42.7 ms is a lower bound.

What it says for kernel work: 91 % of the measured pass is in two families, and both are already near
this part's memory system. The dense projections run at 205 to 220 GB/s of the 232 GB/s the pair
reaches at a five-token batch, and the MoE's marginal cost is 8.75 ms per extra token against
1.6 GiB per node, which is 196 GB/s. So the 1.75x to the reference is not sitting in these kernels,
and a kernel-only campaign with this shape of work has on the order of 10 to 15 % in it, not 75 %.

The 42.7 ms is also 3x under the 125 ms a real pass costs, which is the L2 inflation of the replay
method plus the untimed nodes; it must not be read as a measurement of pass time. It is a share
measurement, and it narrows the sentence above: with both GPUs drawing 44 W through a pass
(`bench/deepseek-v4-tp-coordination.txt`), the pass is not coordination-bound either, so its 125 ms is
kernel time running at roughly half the bandwidth these same tensors reach when measured alone. That
small-batch gap is the one place left where a kernel change can pay, and it is a kernel and batch-shape
problem rather than a protocol one.

--- 2026-09-14, the same graph at one token: what is weight-bound and what is not ---

Exported and replayed twice, `-np 6` and `-np 1`, same tool path, no model load:

| family | 1 token | 6 tokens | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| `MUL_MAT` (dense) | 18.5 ms | 22.3 ms | 1.2x |
| `MUL_MAT_ID` (MoE) | 8.2 ms | 16.5 ms | 2.0x |
| all families | 28.8 ms | 42.7 ms | 1.5x |

Per signature, the split is sharp. The MLA output projections (`ne=[32768,.,1,1]`, 43 nodes) cost
6.2 ms at one token and 6.8 ms at six; the grouped attention input (`ne=[1024,.,8,1]`, 43 nodes) 6.2
against 6.7 ms; the lm head 2.2 against 2.3 ms. Six times the tokens cost these 8 to 10 %, because
their weights are read once per pass whatever the batch carries. The MoE is the only family that
tracks the batch: 5.3 ms at one token against 16.5 ms at six, and even that is sub-linear, 3.1x for
6x the tokens, because a batch's tokens share experts.

That is the shape of the fixed cost this arm pays on every pass: reading the dense weights, 5.25 GiB
of the 10.4 GiB a token touches, once per pass and independent of how many tokens the pass verifies.
At 4.50 accepted tokens per pass that is 18 to 22 ms of every pass spent on bytes that do not change
with the batch, which is why the arm's c1 rate sits where it does and why the single-Spark 2.72 bpw
arm is only 10 % ahead despite reading 40 % fewer bytes: the dense share of its traffic is also
per-pass rather than per-token, and its MoE share is already shared across the batch.

--- 2026-09-14, how the dense-bytes lever could be exercised, and what is not known about it ---

The measurement above says the largest share of the replay budget belongs to the dense read, and its
size is set by the quant: 7.15 GiB of the file's 144.44 GiB is Q8_0, but those tensors are read in full
on every pass while only 6 of 256 experts are, which makes them about 70 % of the per-token bytes. So
the lever with the largest measured term is quantizing those tensors to 4 bits while leaving the MXFP4
experts alone, and `tools/quantize` can express that: `--tensor-type-file` maps per-tensor-name
patterns to types through `parse_ggml_type`, and the option table carries `MXFP4_MOE`
(`LLAMA_FTYPE_MOSTLY_MXFP4_MOE`, tools/quantize/quantize.cpp:39).

Three things are not established and would need checking before a run is worth its hour of machine
time. Whether the option table's MXFP4_MOE entry has a dedicated per-tensor assignment in the type
selection switch, or falls through to a default. Whether tensors already at the target type are copied
verbatim rather than round-tripped through F32, which decides whether the 137 GiB of experts come out
of the requantize unchanged or with a second generation of quantization noise. And what the Q8_0 to
4-bit change costs in quality on the attention projections, which the two greedy gates in
`scripts/bench-spark.py` are far too weak to measure.

Deliberately not run: it changes the served artifact rather than the engine, the quality cost is
unmeasured, and it is a model-level change rather than a llama.cpp one. Recorded here so the decision
is a decision with numbers attached rather than an open question.

--- 2026-09-14, the MMVQ parameter table is not a lever either ---

The dense and MoE matvecs at this batch size run on MMVQ, and MMVQ takes its launch parameters from a
per-architecture table. This pair is a GB10, which has its own (`MMVQ_PARAMETERS_GB10`, selected in
`get_device_table_id` in `ggml/src/ggml-cuda/mmvq.cu`). `patches/0005` is exactly this kind of work for
RDNA3 single-token dispatch, so the table was worth one cheap test. Made switchable at runtime
(`GGML_MMVQ_TABLE=<index>`, a local change, reverted after) and two tables measured on the real shapes,
same replay path, no model load:

| op | device table (GB10) | table 1 (TURING) |
|---|---|---|
| `attn_wo_a` q8_0, six tokens, 43 nodes | 161.1 us | 155.3 us |
| `ffn_moe_gate` mxfp4, 6 of 256, six tokens | 189.5 us | 193.8 us |

Mixed signs at 2 to 4 %, so the parameter table is not where the small-batch gap lives and is not worth
a 17-minute load to chase. Reverted; `mmvq.cu` matches the published source on all three trees
(md5 `dfb59c554303602b3e284309259f2ae1`).

--- 2026-09-14, the requantize was built and served: no gain, and the output changes ---

The lever above was run, with the user's go-ahead. Recipe, verified with `--dry-run` first:
`llama-quantize --allow-requantize --tensor-type-file tt.txt <UD-Q4_K_XL shard 1> all4bit.gguf Q4_K 20`
where `tt.txt` maps `ffn_gate_exps`, `ffn_up_exps` and `ffn_down_exps` to `mxfp4` so the experts keep
their format and only the dense tensors drop. The dry run confirmed the assignment: experts unchanged
at mxfp4, attention and shared-expert tensors from q8_0 to q4_K (a few upgraded to q6_K by the k-quant
mixture). Model 147905 MiB (4.36 BPW) to 144517 MiB (4.26 BPW), and the whole file went from 144.44 GiB
to 141.1 GiB in 2.7 minutes of quantize, because the already-mxfp4 experts are copied rather than
round-tripped.

Measured on the same configuration as the best c1 arm (two slots, f16 KV, DSpark):

| c1, three reps | median | range | spread |
|---|---|---|---|
| UD-Q4_K_XL (q8_0 dense) | 40.4 (two runs) | 39.0 - 41.1 | 3 - 5 % |
| all4bit (q4_K dense) | 40.9 | 40.3 - 40.9 | 1.5 % |

So about 1 %, inside the spread, for 28 % fewer weight bytes per token. That is the small-batch
latency finding confirmed from the other direction: at one to six tokens per pass this engine is
limited by latency, not by the bytes it moves, so halving the dense traffic buys nothing measurable.

And it is not free. The greedy gate changed: `gate_9x8` still answers `72, 9x9`, but `gate_france`
answers ` Paris.", "The capital of` where both the reference and the q8_0 model answer
` Paris. The capital of Spain`. One gate is a weak test, but it is the test this repo uses, and it moved.

Verdict: not adopted. The artifact stays UD-Q4_K_XL. The recipe and the result are recorded so that the
byte-shaped lever is closed with a measurement rather than with an argument, and `all4bit.gguf` is kept
on spark1 (141.1 GiB) in case a long-context test ever wants it, where the bytes would matter more.

--- 2026-09-14, reconciling the two nulls: dense is latency-limited, MoE is byte-limited ---

Two measurements that looked contradictory and are not. The requantise took 28 % off the per-token
weight bytes for about 1 % of throughput (section above), which says the pass is not byte-limited. The
same graph exported at one token instead of six costs 28.8 ms against 42.7 ms, which says the pass does
scale with something the batch carries.

The batch carries two different things. The dense projections are read once per pass whatever the batch
holds, and their cost is unchanged by removing their bytes, so they are limited by latency rather than
by bandwidth: the replay puts the dense family at 22.3 ms for six tokens and 18.5 ms for one, while
their traffic at 232 GB/s is about 11 ms per node. That is roughly 2x off the memory system with the
bytes already paid for. The MoE is the opposite: its traffic grows with the batch because each token
brings its own experts, and it moves those bytes at 196 GB/s per node, which is 84 % of what the part
reaches.

So the arithmetic for a pass is one latency-limited family and one bandwidth-limited family, and the
levers are correspondingly different. For the MoE, fewer bytes per expert or fewer experts per token
(the requantise did not touch it, which is why it showed almost nothing). For the dense family, the fix
is latency: fewer ops, fused ops, or more memory-level parallelism inside the existing kernels, and the
op budget already says 91 % of its time is in two families that cannot be made much cheaper per byte.

One caveat that has to be checked before anyone acts on the dense number: the replay times one
occurrence of each signature as its own graph launch, so its per-op floor near 10 us may be an artefact
of the method rather than a property of the kernel. A dense kernel that takes 10 us under the replay and
0.2 us inside the real graph would make the dense family bandwidth-bound after all, and the requantise
null would then need another explanation.
